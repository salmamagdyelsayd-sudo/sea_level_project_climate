Academic summary

Global coastal zones face an escalating convergence of climate-induced sea-level rise (SLR), storm surges, extreme high tides, and local land subsidence. These pressures create long-term sustainability risks for human settlements and natural ecosystems (Wahl et al., 2018; McBean & Huang, 2017). Coastal areas hold dense cities, industrial centers, and critical infrastructure, with population densities about three times the global inland mean (Bosello & De Cian, 2014). Assessing these hazards means combining risk frameworks, satellite Earth Observation, two-dimensional hydrodynamic models, and macroeconomic assessments (Xu et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2021).

SLR rates and subsidence

Between 1993 and 2015, global mean relative sea-level rise averaged 2.5 mm/yr. Because people concentrate in rapidly subsiding deltas and alluvial plains, coastal inhabitants experience 7.8 to 9.9 mm/yr, up to four times faster than climate-driven SLR alone. Subsidence accounts for 51% to 70% of that rise (Nicholls et al., 2021b). In China, tide gauges show a 90 mm increase over 30 years, with rates of 4.1 mm/yr in the Pearl River Delta (1959–2011) and 2.6 to 3.0 mm/yr in the Yangtze River Delta (1980–2011) (Zhao et al., 2021). The IPCC AR5 projected a likely upper-bound mean rise of 98 cm by 2100 under RCP8.5, and 2 m or more is a plausible high-end risk (Wahl et al., 2018). The Antarctic ice sheet holds enough ice to raise seas by 58 m if fully melted (McBean & Huang, 2017). Internal Climate Variability adds 2 to 17 cm of irreducible uncertainty globally, which amplifies forced rise by 20% to over 50% in major coastal cities by 2100 (Becker et al., 2023).

Inundation and exposure

Low-elevation coastal zones below 10 m hold about 10% of the world’s people, 768 million in 2015, and 235 million lived in the 100-year flood plain (Wahl et al., 2018; Nicholls et al., 2021b). Coastal urban areas will hold 12.4% of the world’s population by 2060 (McBean & Huang, 2017). In 2005, 136 port cities above one million people held about US$3,000 billion in exposed assets, roughly 5% of Gross World Product (Bosello & De Cian, 2014). In the conterminous US, 20 large and 160 medium municipalities have low-lying land connected to the ocean; on average 9.7% of their land lies at or below 1 m and 35.8% at or below 6 m. In Miami, New Orleans, and Virginia Beach, over 90% lies at or below 6 m (Weiss et al., 2011). In Xiamen, extreme sea levels of 4.75 to 5.86 m would flood 11,600 to 17,100 ha and expose 440,000 to 720,000 residents (Xu et al., 2016). By 2100 under RCP8.5, Manila, Bangkok, Ho Chi Minh City, Yangon, Kolkata, and Chennai become hotspots, where ICV adds at least 20% to 30% of expected rise (Becker et al., 2023).

Urban and infrastructure impacts

Transport networks, industry, and waste facilities along shorelines face tidal flooding, storm surge, and erosion (Griggs, 2020; Nicholls et al., 2021a). LaGuardia, JFK, and Newark airports were flooded during Superstorm Sandy in 2012, and several other US airports are designated vulnerable to high water (Griggs, 2020). Europe has 350,000 to 500,000 historical landfills, about 90% built before modern waste rules, and the US has 945 Superfund sites exposed to climate impacts. In Florida, 1,099 landfills sit in the 100-year coastal flood zone, projected to reach 1,642 by 2100 under RCP8.5 (Nicholls et al., 2021a). Xiamen’s direct losses range from 4,744 to 10,590 million RMB (1.69% to 3.76% of 2012 city GDP), mostly buildings and indoor property, plus 2,537 to 4,175 million RMB of indirect industrial losses (Xu et al., 2016). Market adjustments are a substantial part of total disaster exposure (Bosello & De Cian, 2014).

Adaptation strategies

Good adaptation combines engineering, nature-based protection, land-use planning, and policy fitted to local conditions (Wahl et al., 2018; Bosello & De Cian, 2014). San Francisco and Oakland airports plan barriers against 0.6 to 0.9 m of added rise (Griggs, 2020). Controlling groundwater extraction and managing drainage can keep 20 to 35 million people out of the coastal flood plain by 2050 (Nicholls et al., 2021b). In southern England, defending 80 priority landfills for 100 years costs about £150 million, versus £4.3 billion to remove the waste (Nicholls et al., 2021a). Pairing DInSAR and satellite altimetry with 2D flood models gives decision-makers precise maps of land deformation and shoreline change (Zhao et al., 2021). Accounting for ICV and high-end scenarios in integrated risk frameworks prevents under-engineered defenses (Becker et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2016).
